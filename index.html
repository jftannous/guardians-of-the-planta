How does an NLR “see” a pathogen?

There are two broad strategies: direct recognition and indirect recognition, and indirect recognition splits into two models.

Direct recognition

The NLR itself physically binds the effector — through its LRR or another domain, including an integrated domain fused into the receptor.

Indirect: guard–guardee

The NLR (the guard) monitors a host protein (the guardee) that effectors normally modify to promote infection. It senses the modification, not the effector itself.

Indirect: decoy

The NLR monitors a host protein (the decoy) that mimics an effector target but has no role of its own — it exists to bait the effector and trigger immunity when modified.

From perception to cell death

Resistosome Assembly PRACTICAL · 100 pts each

Two more activation routes

NRC network — “activation‑and‑release”

A sensor NLR (e.g. Rx, Bs2) perceives its effector and relays a signal to a downstream helper NRC — but the sensor itself never oligomerizes. Only the helper (NRC2/3/4) forms the resistosome and moves to the plasma membrane.

NRG1 / ADR1 network — TIR‑NLR relay

Activated TIR‑NLRs form tetrameric resistosomes that act as enzymes, producing small molecules sensed by EDS1–PAD4 or EDS1–SAG101 complexes. Those complexes then trigger oligomerization of the CCR helpers ADR1 and NRG1, which act as calcium channels.
